## In short

Today a knockdown is a pause of about four seconds. This plan makes it a short exchange with a choice on each side: the fighter left standing follows up, and the one on the ground answers. I propose calling it **Downed play**.

- **The one standing** stabs, slashes low or kicks; or takes a victory lap; or waits, as he does now.
- **The one down** rolls clear and ends face up, guards from his back with shield or sword, and gets up facing his enemy. A roll into the legs can bring the other down.
- **The choices beat each other.** A half roll beats a stab, a slash beats a half roll, the shield or a longer roll beats a slash, and going for the standing leg beats a kick. That falls out of sizes and speeds we can measure.
- **One new piece carries most of it**: a ground score, which is our rebuilt-from-parts method for a body lying down. The rest reuses the attack maker, the shield and parry, step 7's reader and the leg rules.
- **Ten steps**, attacks first. It goes after the dance and after stumbles, before the fight of six.

Nothing here is built or tried. It is a plan from the code, your three packs and what others have done.

## Decided on 16 November

You answered all seven questions and added two things that change the plan.

- **The name** is Downed play.
- **A kick** is answered as a stab or a slash is, plus the roll into the standing leg.
- **Two-handed stabs** are left out for now.
- **A victory lap** counts toward demoralising the enemy. How is worked out later; the game has no morale yet.
- **A hard kick to the head** makes him go limp, as a rag doll, for a few seconds.
- **Near** is 3 m.

**Added: how the fight is going decides lap or attack.** A fighter who is far ahead of the man he has put down is more likely to take a victory lap. One who is losing, or in a close fight, is more likely to attack him. This sits on top of his character.

**Added: Downed play is a skill, not a diversion.** How a fighter handles being down, and how he handles a man who is down, is part of how good he is and of what makes him himself. Each fighter gets levers of his own for it:

- how soon he comes to;
- how fast he rolls, and how far he judges he must;
- how well he guards from his back;
- how fast he gets up;
- how well he reads the follow-up;
- how well he turns the tables.

**Turning the tables** becomes a step of its own. The plan had one way: the roll into the legs. I am adding two: striking as he rises, and a kick from his back at the legs of the man standing over him. Virtua Fighter calls the first a rising attack.

## Where this goes

After the dance and after stumbles, and before the fight of six. You asked me to choose, and that is the order that does not cram it in.

1. **The dance** (the fight plan's step 8). Two fighters, each with gestures in a pattern of his own, each reading the other.
2. **Stumbles** (the fight plan's step 9), and with it how often a fighter goes down.
3. **Downed play**, steps 0 to 8, with turning the tables added.
4. **Back into the fight of six** (the fight plan's step 10), with Downed play's last step inside it.

Three reasons for not starting it now.

- **Its levers need a home that the dance builds.** Today the movement levers are one set for everybody. The dance is where each fighter gets his own. Your point that Downed play should set fighters apart needs that first.
- **"How the fight is going" does not exist yet.** Lap or attack now depends on who is ahead. The dance is where two fighters first have a fight that can be going well or badly.
- **Stumbles feed it.** A living fighter went down once in the last 60 s fight of six. How often fighters go down is settled in stumbles. Building ten steps for something that happens once a minute, before that is settled, would be building blind.

## What you asked for

When one fighter knocks another down, the two play out a short scene on the ground instead of the fallen one simply lying there and getting up.

| The one standing does | The one on the ground answers |
| --- | --- |
| Stabs at him, with one hand or two | Face down: rolls half over, out from under the point, and stops face up. Face up: blocks or parries with sword or shield as he stands up |
| Slashes at him, bent low enough to reach | Face down: rolls further, because a slash covers more ground, and still stops face up. Face up: blocks or parries as he stands up |
| Kicks him, in the body or the head, hard enough to knock him about | The same as for a stab or a slash (my assumption: decision 3) |
| Walks off with his back turned, comes round in an arc and walks back, swinging his sword slowly in the air | Gets up normally |
| Circles him with a straight arm, the point of his sword on him all the way round | Gets up normally |

Two rules sit across all of it.

- **Up from his back, not from his front.** With an enemy near, a fighter only ever gets up from facing up, so he can see who is over him. He gets up from face down only when he has been knocked well clear.
- **A roll into the legs takes them.** If his roll carries him into the standing fighter's legs, it can roll them out from under him.

You also gave three packs of recordings to rebuild from: kicks, rolls and get-ups.

## What the game does today

Today a knockdown is a pause: the fallen fighter lies still, the winner waits, and about four seconds after he was hit (0.7 s falling, 1.4 s lying, 2 s getting up) the fight carries on as if nothing had happened.

- **Going down.** A fighter falls when a blow is more than his legs can catch: a leg swept, a body thrown or borne down. The fall is physical, with muscle: he bends, puts a hand out, keeps his head up.
- **Lying.** He lies about 1.4 s (1.1 to 1.7), longer if he was thrown, never more than 2.5 s. He does nothing in that time and cannot defend.
- **Getting up.** About 2 s, by one of three routes: from his back, from his front, or from a side. He turns up to 35 degrees toward his enemy as he rises. The get-up is made of key poses set by hand, not rebuilt from recordings.
- **The winner.** The fight's rule for choosing a target skips anyone on the ground. With no one else to fight, its own note says: "it waits for one to get up".
- **A blow on a man who is down** does land: it counts as a hit and knocks the part it struck. Nobody throws one on purpose.

Three things already in the game turn out to matter.

- **A leg carrying all the weight is swept easily.** A leg holding the whole body, the other foot in the air, goes from a knock of 1.2 m/s. Both feet down, it takes 4 m/s. So a man caught on one leg, kicking or stepping, is the one a roll can bring down.
- **A defender already reads a blow coming**: where it will land, the blade's path, and how long until it arrives. The man on the ground can use the same reading to decide which way to roll.
- **Getting up with a shield already keeps the shield arm free**, toward the enemy, with the sword hand on the ground. That is most of what guarding while standing up needs.

One honest number: knockdowns of a living fighter are rare right now. The last 60 s fight of six had one. This feature shows only as often as fighters go down, so how often they should is part of the plan.

## The design: one exchange on the ground

A knockdown starts a short exchange with two choices in it: the standing fighter picks a follow-up, and the fallen one, if he has his wits back in time, picks an answer to it.

1. **He goes down.** The fall we have, about 0.7 s.
2. **He comes to.** A fighter is dazed for a moment after landing: longer after a hard fall, shorter for a tough fighter. Until then he can do nothing. This replaces today's fixed 1.4 s of lying still.
3. **The standing fighter chooses**: attack him, take a victory lap, or wait as he does today. His character decides which, and whether anyone else is threatening him.
4. **The fallen one reads it and answers.** This is step 7's reading over again. The walk in, the sword going up or the leg going back are the tell. A fighter who reads starts his answer on the tell. One who only reacts starts when the blow is already coming.
5. **It resolves.** The blow lands, misses or is blocked; or the roll takes the attacker's legs. Then the fallen one gets up facing him and the fight goes on, or he does not get up.

**Why the choices are real ones.** Each attack has a size and a speed, and each answer covers a distance in a time. From the recordings and from our own blows:

| The follow-up | What it is like | Beaten by | Beats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stab | Narrow and quick. Hurts most | A half roll he read in time: a quarter turn already carries him 0.35 to 0.4 m in about 0.45 s. A guard from his back | A man still dazed, or one who only reacts |
| Low slash | Wide, and slower because he has to bend down to it | A long roll begun early (1.2 m takes 1.5 s). A shield from his back | The half roll: 0.6 m is still inside its sweep |
| Kick | Quickest (about 0.5 s), no bending, hurts least. Moves him, can turn him over, knocks a man who is half up back down | A roll into the leg he is standing on | A man trying to stand, or lying behind his shield |

So a stab is answered by a half roll, a half roll by a slash, a slash by rolling further or by the shield, and a kick by going for the leg. Nobody has to type that in. How far he must roll is read off the blade's path along the ground, which a defender already sees. A stab's path is a spot; a slash's is a stripe. That is your "keep rolling a bit more" rule, worked out by the bodies.

These times are from the recordings and from blows standing up. What they come to on the ground is measured in the first step, before anything is built on them.

**A kick is built the way a sword blow is.** The recording is taken apart into the kicking leg, the standing leg, the hips and the arms that balance. The kicking leg is then aimed at a part of him, body or head, as the sword arm is aimed today. The foot is the weapon, and the blow is found the way a blade's is. How hard it knocks him comes from the foot's speed and the leg's weight. My estimate is that a big kick carries about half the push that fells a standing man; that is enough to slide and turn a lying body, and it is measured in its own step.

**Body or head.** A kick to the body moves him and can turn him over. A hard kick to the head makes him go limp for a few seconds (decided 16 November), which is a reason to choose it. A blow on a man lying on the ground hurts by the rules we have; he cannot give with it, and that is already how those rules work.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Walk off and back | Turns his back, walks about 3 m, comes round in an arc and walks back, swinging his sword slowly in the air | Our walk along a short route. The swing is the blade bluff we have, made big and slow, laid on the walk as every posture is |
| Circle and point | Walks round him at about 1.8 m with a straight arm, the point on him all the way | The circling we have, with one new arm lever: the sword arm laid on the line to a point |

## The fallen fighter's side

He gets four things to do on the ground: roll, swing round on his back, guard from his back, and get up. All four end the same way, on his back or on his feet, facing the man who put him down.

**Rolling clear (from face down).**

- **Half a roll** takes him 0.6 m in 0.7 to 0.8 s and leaves him face up. This is the answer to a stab.
- **A roll and a half** takes him 1.8 m and also leaves him face up. This is the answer to a slash. It takes about 2.3 s at the recordings' quick pace, so it only works begun early.
- **Which way.** Away from the blade's path. When both ways are clear, the way that brings his shield up on top of him as he stops.
- **Sword and shield through the roll.** The recordings carry a rifle in both hands. Ours keep the shield in against the chest as he goes over that arm, and the sword out along the ground clear of his own body. The game already measures a blade inside its owner's body, so this is checked, not hoped.

One thing the recordings showed: he travels 1.2 m for each full turn, which is what a body about 38 cm across (19 cm from its middle to the ground) covers rolling without sliding. So how far he goes follows from how far he turns. We do not set a distance; we set the turn.

**Swinging round on his back.** Once face up, he turns on his back so his feet point at the enemy and his head is away. Fighting games treat this as the safe way to lie, and every stand-up taught for real starts from the back.

**Guarding from his back.** Lying face up and all through the get-up, his shield goes to meet a blow as it does when he stands. A fighter with no shield parries with the sword and posts on the other hand. He guards worse than on his feet, because the arm reaches from a shoulder on the ground. A heavy blow caught while half up can put him down again, by the weight rules we have.

**Getting up.**

| When | Route | Rebuilt from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An enemy is near and he is face up | Up from his back, shield arm free toward the enemy, turned to face him | Getting Up (the 2.7 s one) |
| An enemy is near and he is face down or on a side | Half a roll first, away from the enemy, then up from his back | A roll, then Getting Up |
| Nobody is near (I propose: nobody within 3 m) | Up from however he lies, from his front too | Getting Up (2) |
| He is badly hurt | The same routes, slower | Standing Up, Getting Up (1) |
| During a victory lap | Normally: unhurried, no rolling for cover | The same get-ups |

**Rolling into the legs.** If his roll reaches the standing fighter's legs, the rule for a struck leg decides the rest. A man on both feet staggers. A man caught on one leg, in the middle of a kick or a step, goes down. Then there are two fighters on the ground, and whoever comes to first is the one standing.

## How it is built on our system

One new piece carries most of this: a ground score. Everything else is something we have, pointed at a man on the ground.

**The ground score.** Today a movement or a blow is taken apart into each body part's own track, in a frame that assumes he is upright: hips over feet, feet held to the floor. Lying down, that frame does not fit. A ground score is the same idea for a body on the ground. Its parts:

- where his hips are on the ground, and how high;
- which way his body's length points;
- how far he is turned over, from face down to face up and round again;
- how far his trunk is raised, from lying to sitting;
- the bend along his spine;
- each limb as a direction on the part it hangs from, so it rolls with him;
- which parts bear on the ground at each moment: a shoulder, a hip, a knee, an elbow, a hand, a foot. A part that bears does not slide, as a planted foot does not.

A roll, the swing round on his back, sitting up and all four get-ups are scores of this kind. A get-up is a ground score that ends where the standing one begins. It has levers as the made movement has: how far he turns, the pace, how tucked his knees are, how his arms are carried.

**From however he lies.** The get-up we have already blends from the pose the fallen body is really in, each part on its own clock, and the physical body follows. Ground acts use that same machinery. Only where the poses come from changes: from a score rebuilt from recordings, in place of key poses set by hand.

| Piece | What it reuses | What is new |

**Measured, not typed in.** How far a roll carries him comes from how far he turns. How far he has to roll comes from the blade's path. How hard a kick knocks him comes from the foot's speed and the leg's weight. Whether a leg goes comes from the leg rule. Whether he is face up comes from how his own body lies.

**Every step on a switch, in a lab of its own.** A new "Downed lab": a swordsman and a target, buttons that put the target down on its back, its front or a side, then buttons for each follow-up and each answer, with old and new side by side where there is an old.

## Who chooses what: the numbers per fighter

Five numbers per fighter decide the whole exchange, and two of them he has already from step 7.

| Number | Whose | What it does |

Two rules sit over the numbers. He takes a lap only when no other enemy is close enough to threaten him. And he drops a follow-up the moment someone else attacks him.

**What needs what.** Steps 1, 2 and 8 stand alone. Step 3 is the foundation for 4, 5, 6 and 7. Step 6 needs 1 to 5. Step 9 needs all of it, and belongs with the fight plan's own last step, back into the fight of six.

## Risks and what is not known

Nothing in this plan has been tried. These are the places it is most likely to go wrong.

- **A made blow may not aim at the ground.** Our blows are made for a standing target. A thrust turned steeply down may be further than the recorded arm can be turned. If so, the stab needs a recording of its own, or a score built for it. Step 0 answers this.
- **The ground score is the big build.** It is a new frame and a new rule for what bears on the ground. Everything the fallen fighter does waits on it.
- **A shield under a rolling body.** The board is 54 cm across. Rolling over that arm may wedge or lift him. He may have to roll only the way that keeps it on top, or lift the arm over.
- **A physical body on the ground follows a pose less well than a standing one.** The get-up we have still moves parts at up to 5.8 m/s in its first moments. A roll may look dragged before it looks rolled.
- **A kick stands him on one leg.** Our movement keeps feet planted by rule. A kick has to hold the standing foot and let the other go, and the made movement may fight that.
- **Knockdowns are rare today**: one in the last 60 s fight of six. In a real fight this plays only as often as someone goes down. How often that should be is its own question.
- **A free hit on a man who is down may end fights too fast.** Step 9 watches how long fights last, before and after.
- **The tell on the ground is untested.** Step 7's reader has only been tried on a blade dip and on counted fakes. A walk in with the sword going up is a different kind of tell.
- **Six fighters.** Someone else will interrupt, and a friend may stand over a fallen man. None of that is designed here; it comes with step 9.
- **The recordings are bare-handed or carry a rifle.** There is no reference for a stab downward, a low slash, or a block from the back. Those are worked out, not copied.
- **Not looked at**: Exanima and Half Sword, the two physics sword games nearest to ours.

## Decisions for you

All seven were answered on 16 November; the answers are in "Decided on 16 November" near the top. The questions as they were put:

1. **The name.** I propose **Downed play**, with **the follow-up** for what the standing fighter does and **the wake-up** for what the fallen one does. Others: Ground play, The wake-up game, Knockdown scenes.
2. **When.** I propose steps 0 to 5 next, then the dance (the fight plan's step 8), then steps 6 to 9. Steps 6 and 9 lean on the dance; the first six do not.
3. **What answers a kick.** You gave answers for stabs and slashes. I gave a kick the same answers, plus the roll into the standing leg. Say if a kick should be answered differently.
4. **Two hands.** I kept the two-handed stab to fighters with no shield on that arm. The other way is to let a shield fighter do it with the shield hanging from his forearm.
5. **Does a lap pay.** To start, a victory lap pays nothing and is there for character. It could give him his breath back, or bring the other up rattled.
6. **A kick to the head dazes him again.** Yes or no. It gives the kick a use of its own.
7. **How near is near.** I used 3 m for the rule that he only gets up from his back when an enemy is near.

Three recordings would help and are not required: a stab straight down, with one hand and with two; a low slash at something on the ground; and a man on his back fending off blows as he gets up.
