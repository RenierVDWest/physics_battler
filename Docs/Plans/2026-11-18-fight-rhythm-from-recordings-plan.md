## Bottom line

A fight rhythm becomes a **movement style made from recordings**, the same way an attack is now made from a recorded strike. Recordings are split into body-part tracks, changed with levers, and put back together on top of the walking you already like. Nothing in the walk is thrown away: planted feet, the physical lower body and the balance checks stay as they are.

Three new things carry the plan:

1. **A style is a pack.** A movement style is a named set of recordings (base, walks, runs, turns, hops) plus its levers. Give me a new pack and you get a new way of moving.
2. **Attacks start from wherever the body is.** Today a made attack starts from the recorded guard. It will start from the pose the fighter is in, so a blow can come out of a hop, a circle step or the capoeira sway without a snap.
3. **Postures and openings come from the same parts.** A feint is a real attack's wind-up, pulled back. A fake step is a lunge's legs under a quiet upper body. What a posture leaves open is measured, not hand-marked.

Two rhythms are built to prove it: **standard** (close to what you have, but loose on its feet) and **capoeira** (the sway as a base, with the sword riding the arms).

Nothing is built yet. This is the plan for your comments.

## What you asked for

- **No two fighters standing face to face trading blows.** They should do the dance real fighters do: close, back off, circle, fake, strike.
- **A rhythm has three pieces.**
  - **Base:** the walking system, rebuilt so new clips give new movement styles.
  - **Postures:** bursts of 0.3 to 1.2 s. Lower body: fake steps, small leaps forward, back and to the sides. Upper body: fake attacks, bluffs with the sword, dodges.
  - **Openings:** a posture leaves a part exposed for a moment, and the other fighter waits for it instead of attacking on a timer.
- **Two rhythms to build the system on.** Standard, from the walking packs. Capoeira, from a pack with no swords in it, joined to the sword attacks.
- **For capoeira,** the arms keep their fluid movement with the sword never still, and an attack grows out of wherever the arm happens to be.
- **The current battle is scrapped.** The made attacks are the new basis for the real combat system.

**Must not be lost:** feet that stay planted, realistic steps, the physical lower body, awareness of the centre of gravity, the ability to stumble.

**On stumbles, since you asked:** partly there. A body thrown back by a blow already takes catch steps and stays up if its legs can catch it, or falls if they cannot. But those catch steps are the ordinary upright walk played fast, so it does not yet look like a stumble. A proper stumble is still to do, and it is a step in this plan.

What the ginga looks like in numbers:

- **The feet** step 1.2 to 1.9 m apart, and each stays planted 0.3 to 0.9 s.
- **The hips** sway up to 1.5 m side to side and ride 10 to 25% lower than standing.
- **The hips turn** up to 45 degrees each way. That is a wind-up, for free, on every beat.
- **The chest** leans 18 to 42 degrees.
- **The hands** swing 0.6 m to either side of the chest at 2.5 to 5 m/s. They are never still.

## What the research says

### How fighters move

From fencing coaching, which has studied this longest:

- **Distance is the game.** Fighters hover at the edge of reach, where neither can hit without a step. Most of a bout is spent managing that gap, not striking.
- **Preparation steps set up the blow; they are not the blow.** Small advances and half retreats bring the fighter to the distance where one action will land.
- **The moment to attack is a moment in the other's movement.** The classic cues: the opponent steps forward, is caught mid-step, makes a big slow action with the weapon, or is recovering.
- **The decision is made by the time the front foot lands.** If the opponent has moved away by then, the fighter aborts and resets in balance.
- **Broken rhythm wins.** A half retreat that turns into a lunge with no pause catches the opponent mid-step. Used too often, it stops surprising.

This maps onto the tempo states you already designed (Gather, Probe, Press, Shell, Counter). It also says what an opening is: mostly a moment in the other fighter's own movement.

### How the ginga works

- It is a **constant triangular step**: one foot goes back as the opposite hand comes up to cover the face, the feet come level, then the other side.
- Its purpose is to **never be a still target** and to keep the body wound up so a kick, sweep or dodge can come out at any instant with no separate setup.
- **The arms move all the time**, up and down and inside to outside, in step with the feet. Old masters describe this as useful against weapons.
- It **shows or hides intent**. A player bluffs with shoulders, hips, hands and head. This is your posture idea, already built into the style.
- It follows a **beat**. Its speed is set by the music.

### How games join movements together

- **Layers.** Lower body and upper body are driven separately and joined at the spine. We already do this: the body-part tracks are separate.
- **Carrying motion through a switch.** When one movement hands over to another, the best-looking method keeps the speed each joint already had and eases it into the new movement, instead of fading between two poses. This is how a moving arm can flow into an attack with no snap.
- **Stride and foot locking.** Steps are stretched to fit the speed asked for and a planted foot is held where it landed. The walk already does both.

## The idea

### What already exists, and stays

The walk already works from recordings. Steering asks for a speed and a facing; the walk picks and mixes recorded step cycles to match, stretches the stride to fit, and holds each planted foot where it landed. Near an enemy it stands planted until a step's worth of ground is asked for, then takes a real step. The physical lower body and the balance checks sit on top. **None of this changes.**

### What is new

**1. A movement style is a pack with levers.**

Today the clips the walk uses are wired in by name. They become a *style*: a named set of recordings, each with a job.

| Job | Standard | Capoeira |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base (what it does when holding ground) | Guard stance, stepping when needed | The ginga, stepping all the time |
| Slow travel | Guard walk, strafes | Ginga forward, backward, sideways |
| Fast travel | Brisk walk, run | Run (borrowed from standard at first) |
| Turns | Turn clips | The ginga's own turning |
| Lower-body postures | Hops, fake steps, stance switch | Esquivas, stance switch, kick fakes |
| Upper-body postures | Sword feints and bluffs | Arm flow, sword feints |

Each style also carries levers, the way an attack style does: how big the steps are, how low it rides, how fast its beat is, how much the arms swing.

**2. The body is driven in layers that can come from different recordings.**

The attack work already splits a recording into tracks: hips and legs, chest on hips, head, each arm. Movement uses the same split.

| Layer | Normally from | Can be taken over by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legs and hips | The base or the travel | A lower-body posture, an attack's footwork, a stumble |
| Chest and head | The base | An attack, a dodge, a posture |
| Sword arm | The base | An attack, a feint, a parry |
| Other arm | The base | The shield, a posture |

This is what lets a capoeira lower body carry a sword-and-shield attack on top.

**3. Handing a layer over keeps its motion.**

When a layer changes owner (the arm goes from swaying to striking), the new movement starts from the pose and the speed the layer has at that instant, and eases into its own path. Nothing snaps and nothing freezes.

**4. Attacks start from any pose.**

The attack maker today begins every attack in the recorded guard. It will take the fighter's pose now as the start and ease each body part into the attack's wind-up. The planted feet stay where they are until the attack's own step lifts them. By the time the blade lands, the body is fully on the attack's tracks, so the aiming stays as accurate as it is.

It will also know **which moments are good to start from**. A blow that starts when the hips are already turned away is cheaper and looks better than one that has to unwind first. That is added to the cost the chooser already uses.

## Rhythm 1: standard

### The paces

Today a fighter near its enemy only takes small positioning steps. It gets a full set of paces, all on the same planted feet:

| Pace | Speed | Made from | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Step | under 0.5 m/s | What exists: stand, then one real step | Fine positioning at the edge of reach |
| Walk | 1.1 to 1.5 m/s | Guard walk, strafes, walk back | Circling, easing in and out |
| Brisk | 2 to 3 m/s | The guard walk with a longer stride and quicker beat at first; a jog clip when we have one | Closing or opening the gap with the guard still up |
| Run | about 4.2 m/s | Guard run, forward and back | Covering ground, getting out fast |
| Leap | 0.4 to 2.6 m in one go | The 2.6 m leap, shortened; attack lunges | Bursting in, springing out |

### The dance

What decides the pace is the gap to the enemy. Reach is how far a fighter's blow lands with its lunge.

| Where | What the fighter does |
| --- | --- |
| Far (well beyond reach) | Comes in at a run or brisk, slowing as it nears |
| The edge (just outside the enemy's reach) | **The dance happens here.** Circles in spells, changes direction, steps in and out, throws postures, watches for an opening |
| Inside reach | Strikes or defends, then leaves. It does not stay |
| Too close | Backs out fast or angles off to a side |

Rules that keep it from becoming two statues:

- **No standing still inside reach.** After a blow lands, misses or is blocked, the fighter steps out or angles off.
- **In and out.** At the edge it keeps stepping a little in and a little out, on a beat, with the beat broken now and then.
- **Circling in spells.** A few steps one way, a pause or a posture, then the same way or the other.
- **The tempo states steer it** (your existing design): gathering, it holds just outside reach; probing, it steps in with fakes; pressing, it closes and strikes in bursts; shelled up, it gives ground; countering, it answers straight after a defence.

### Its postures

**Lower body**

| Posture | What it looks like | Made from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fake lunge | A sharp half step forward and back, as if attacking | The forehand cut's lunge legs, shortened, with the upper body staying in guard |
| Hop in / hop out | A small two-footed spring forward or back, 0.3 to 0.6 m | The leap clip, shortened |
| Side hop | A quick spring to a side | The strafe run's first step |
| Stance switch | The back foot passes forward | The rising cut's passing step |
| Drop | A sudden dip of the hips, as if loading | The crouch clip, partial |

**Upper body**

| Posture | What it looks like | Made from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feint | The start of a real attack, pulled back | A made attack's wind-up, stopped before the drive and eased back. It is the real attack's start, so it cannot be told apart until late |
| Blade bluff | The point dips, circles or flicks | The guard idles' own sword movement, enlarged |
| Shield press | The shield jabs forward | The block clip's raise, partial |
| Guard change | High guard to low, or side to side | Two guard idles |

Lower and upper postures can run at the same time, because they own different layers: a hop in with a feint on top reads as a committed attack.

### Its levers

Step size, beat, bounce, stance width, crouch, lean, guard height, hop size, and restlessness (how often it throws postures).

## Rhythm 2: capoeira

### The ginga as the base

The ginga is a step cycle that goes nowhere: left foot back, feet level, right foot back, over and over. The walk already knows how to play step cycles with planted feet, so the ginga goes in as **a walk whose speed is zero**. That gives it everything the walk has: each foot locked while it is down, real steps, the physical legs under it.

- **Travelling.** When steering asks it to move, the ginga forward, backward and sideways recordings are mixed in, the same way walking forward and strafing are mixed today. It sways its way toward the enemy.
- **Far away.** Beyond the dance distance it runs like the standard style and drops into the ginga as it nears.
- **Variety.** The six ginga recordings are different takes on the same step. Moving between them keeps it from looking like a loop.

Its levers:

| Lever | What it changes |
| --- | --- |
| Size | How wide the steps and the sway are. Recorded, the hips sway up to 1.5 m, which is a lot on a board with neighbours. I would start at about two thirds |
| Depth | How low it rides |
| Beat | How fast the cycle goes. Quicker when pressing, slower when gathering |
| Hip turn | How far the hips wind each beat |
| Lean | How far the chest tips |
| Arm flow | How far the arms swing |

### The sword in a body that was recorded without one

- **The sword arm follows the recorded arm.** The hand travels the path it travels in the ginga, relative to the chest, so the sword is never still.
- **The wrist is ours, not the recording's.** The recorded hand was empty, so where it points means nothing for a blade. The wrist is set by rule: point up and a little forward, edge leading the way the arm is swinging. This is the straight-wrist rule you set earlier.
- **The blade is kept out of the body.** It may not pass through the fighter's own head, trunk, legs or the ground. The checks that already keep the sword arm out of the body are used.
- **The other arm stays free.** In the ginga it rises to cover the face on every back step. A shield strapped to it would fight that motion, so I recommend sword only (your decision, below).
- **Hands on the ground.** In most esquivas a hand goes down. It must be the free hand. Where the recording uses the sword hand, the mirrored take is used.

### An attack out of the ginga

This is the hard part. Four things have to line up.

1. **When.** The hips wind up to 45 degrees each way on every beat. The moment the sword-side hip is back is a ready-made wind-up. Attacks that start there cost least, so the chooser will prefer them. An attack can still start at any moment; it just has further to go.
2. **The legs.** Two ways, both using what the attack maker already does:
   - *The attack's own footwork*, starting from where the feet are planted. The forehand's step and push begin from the ginga's stance.
   - *The ginga's legs carry the blow.* The attack's chest and arm are put on the ginga's forward step, the way the thrust was put on the forehand's lunge. The blow lands as the ginga step lands.
3. **The arm.** It flows from wherever it is into the wind-up, keeping the speed it has. Often it is already drawn across the body, which reads as a wind-up.
4. **Afterwards.** The way back eases into the ginga at the beat that matches where the feet ended up, so the sway picks up without a reset.

### Its postures

| Posture | Made from | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Esquiva (duck or slip) | esquiva 1 to 5 | A real dodge under a high cut, and a bluff |
| Stance switch | The ginga's own change of lead | Changes which side is open |
| Kick fake | The first part of a bencao or martelo, pulled back | A threat from below while the sword threatens above |
| Sword feint | A made attack's wind-up, pulled back | As in the standard style |
| Burst of sway | A quicker, bigger ginga for one beat | Draws a reaction |

### Left out for now

Kicks as real attacks (they are on your list for later and change gameplay), and the cartwheels and flips.

## Postures and openings

### What an opening is

A body part is **open** when a blade coming from the attacker can reach it without first meeting the defender's shield, sword or arm. The aim test already works this out for a blow before it is thrown (it is the check that says "expected to meet the shield first").

Openings come from three places:

- **A posture.** A shield press takes the shield off the legs. A feint lifts the sword away from the flank.
- **Movement.** A fighter caught mid-step cannot move that foot. A fighter turning shows its side.
- **An attack.** A committed blow leaves the sword arm out and the guard down until it recovers.

### Openings are measured, not hand-marked

For every posture and every base, the game can run it once against an imaginary attacker in front and note, moment by moment, which parts are open. That gives each posture an **opening map**: for example "left thigh open from 0.35 to 0.75 s". Change a lever or swap a clip and the map is worked out again. Nobody types these in.

### How an attacker takes one

An attack needs time to land: about 0.45 s for the thrust, 0.6 s for the forehand cut, more with a longer wind-up. So seeing an open part is not enough. The attacker has to know the part will **still be open when the blade arrives**.

- **A fighter that reads the posture** recognises it as it starts, knows its opening map, and starts its blow early so the blade arrives inside the window. The attack maker can already land a blow at a set moment.
- **A fighter that does not read it** only reacts to what is open right now. Against a 0.4 s window it arrives late and hits the shield.

That difference is your "some styles read openings better than others". It becomes two numbers per unit: **read** (how likely it is to recognise a posture, per style it has met) and **reaction time**.

Feints work against exactly this. A feint is the true start of an attack, so a defender that reads it reacts, and its reaction is the opening.

### The line that is open

The aim test today stops 27 of 38 blows on a shield because nothing picks an open line. With opening maps, the chooser asks a different question: not "which blow fits this part" but "which part is open, when, and which blow gets there in time". This was the next step on the aimed attacks plan, and it folds into this one.

### The test you described

The dummy holds a shield and uncovers a chosen part for 0.4 s, on a button or on a loop. The swordsman must hit that part inside the window. The panel shows the window, when the blow was started and when it landed. Then the dummy plays real postures instead of the scripted shield, and the same test runs on those.

## Risks

- **The ginga on physical legs.** Its steps are wide and low and the hips swing far from the feet. The legs that hold a guard walk may not hold this without tuning. If they cannot, the size lever comes down until they can.
- **The blade in a flowing arm.** A sword in a hand that was recorded empty will try to pass through the body. The wrist rule and the body check should stop it, but the result may look stiffer than the recording. This is the part most likely to need your eye.
- **Attacks from any pose.** A blow started from an awkward pose may look forced. The cost for a bad start should steer the chooser away from those; where it cannot, the fighter waits a beat.
- **Too much motion.** If everything moves all the time, nothing reads. Postures and openings have to stand out from the base. Restlessness is a lever for that reason.
- **Thin source for standard hops and a brisk pace.** First versions will be shortened and stretched versions of other clips. They may look like it until better clips arrive.
- **I cannot see it move.** As with the attacks, I check numbers and stills; you see the motion.

### From Renier: where the real tells come from (16 November)

The tells are the fighters' own gestures. A fighter's standing is broken up by gestures all the time, and it is their pattern that tells when the attack could come: most of his fake slashes are short, and the one before he really attacks is long.

So reading is recognising a pattern in gestures a fighter makes anyway, not a signal made for the purpose. The blade dip in the shield test only stands in for that. The second part of step 7 and step 8 are built on this: the target plays the real postures in a pattern, and a reader is one who has learned the pattern.
